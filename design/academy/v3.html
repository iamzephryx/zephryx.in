Section one

Contents

Seven units, meant to be taken in order — each one assumes you finished the last. Take a single unit on its own if you already know where your gap is; nobody will stop you.

01
Reconnaissance 14 labs · 12 hrs
Open

External surface, identity discovery, and the unglamorous groundwork that decides whether the next unit takes an hour or a fortnight. You build a target profile starting from one domain name and nothing else.

02
Initial Access 19 labs · 14 hrs
Open

Phishing that survives a modern mail gateway, credential attacks against real lockout policy, and the exposed service somebody published in 2019 and forgot. Then the mail-log rule that catches all three.

Loaders from first principles, what the sensor is genuinely watching, and why your payload died. Including the part most courses leave out — measuring evasion against a real sensor rather than hoping.

Redirectors, profile hygiene, and the operational discipline that keeps an engagement alive past the first week. You build the infrastructure, then burn it deliberately and read back what survived in the logs.

Section two

How it is taught

You attack, then you go and catch yourself

Every lab has two halves. In the first you get in, escalate, and take what you came for. In the second you sit at the analyst's console, query the telemetry your own run produced, and write the rule that would have caught you. The lab does not close until both halves pass.

Most people are genuinely surprised at how loud they were. That surprise is the entire point, and it is the thing you cannot get from a course written by somebody who has only ever been on one side of the glass.

The range is not sterile

Real domain, real users, and a scripted fake workforce generating noise all day. Nothing is staged, so the intended path is never simply the obvious one. Objectives are stated; the route is not. Hints unlock when you ask for them, which keeps being stuck productive rather than merely expensive.

Nothing here is theory I have not been paid for

Every unit started as an engagement or a shift. Where a technique has aged out, it is marked as such rather than quietly left in to pad the syllabus — and where I have no lab for something, the contents page shows the gap instead of claiming coverage.

On pace

Self-paced, but the cohort moves together — there is a live teardown every month and the channel is busiest in the fortnight after it.

On prerequisites

You need to be comfortable in a shell and unafraid of documentation. You do not need prior offensive experience.

On refunds

Finish unit one, and if it is not what you were promised here, write to me and I refund it. No form, no interview.

Section three

Who is teaching it

I'm Zephryx. I'm 23. My paycheck comes from a SOC, where I work as a threat hunter looking for the people who got in without permission. The offensive half is what I do with the rest of my time.

That day job is why this exists. Sitting in the SOC changes how you attack — you learn what actually gets noticed. Most offensive training never mentions that, so people finish it loud and confident, which is the worst possible combination on a real engagement.

The research itself I publish openly at zephryx.in, and I'd rather you read it before you pay me anything. What you're buying here is the range it runs on, the order it's been put into, and my attention at the end.
